Inside AI: From words to predictions

How does AI go from the words you type to a guess about what comes next? Explore four simple stages — and change things yourself to see what happens.

This is a teaching playground, not a real large language model. The tokens and vector map are simplified examples; predictions below are calculated from sentences you can edit.

STAGE 2 OF 4

Pieces become IDs, then vectors

A tokenizer splits text into tokens. Each token has an ID, like a number on a library card. A model can then look up a learned vector — a list of numbers — for that token.

Example vector for a selected piece

Token IDs here are assigned by this page. The numbers shown as vectors are illustrative, not embeddings from a trained model. Real tokenizers may split words into word parts.

STAGE 3 OF 4

How can words be close to each other?

Imagine each word as a dot on a map. In some trained embeddings, words used in similar ways have related positions. Click the words below; then try the famous king–queen relationship.

The axes are imaginary teaching dimensions, not literal gender or royalty axes in a language model.

Explore a word relationship

king − man + woman ≈ queen

This famous analogy can appear approximately in some older word embeddings. It is not a rule that every AI model follows.

STAGE 4 OF 4

What word might come next?

Prediction uses learned patterns and context. This little demo counts what follows a phrase in example sentences. Change the examples, then predict again to see how data changes the result.

This word-count predictor is not a transformer or an LLM. It looks for the last one or two matching words in your examples.

What the examples suggest

Real language models use learned representations and many layers of computation to estimate probabilities for the next token. Our mini model shows the idea of prediction without hiding the evidence.

Your big discovery

AI turns text into tokens and numerical representations. A trained language model uses context and learned patterns to predict the next token. It can still make mistakes.
